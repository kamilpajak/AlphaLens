## Basket 1 — Retail order flow + patient limit orders

### 2024+ scan outcome

Recent microstructure work on retail order flow is heavily exchange-/broker-proprietary (internalization data, detailed order messages), failing your **Public?** filter:

- Most credible post-2024 studies use proprietary broker datasets (Robinhood-style) or exchange feeds that retail quants cannot reasonably subscribe to, and therefore fail filter 3 (replicable from CRSP/Compustat/Polygon/FRED).  
- Where data are public, studies are often short panels (≤5 years), violating filter 1 (sample period ≥ 15 years with post-2010 OOS).

I did not find any 2024–2026 open-access, large-sample paper that:  
- links **retail order flow** to a tradable **patient limit-order strategy**,  
- reports **net of realistic costs** with turnover <100% annually, and  
- applies explicit **multiple-testing correction**.

### Foundational SKIP (background reading)

These are older but foundational to the “retail + limit order” microstructure paradigm; they fail your filters (sample length, proprietary data, or net-return reporting) but are worth grounding:

1. **Barber, Odean, and others on retail trading and performance**  
   - Demonstrate that retail investors are often *liquidity takers* with negative alpha after costs, but also that retail order imbalance can contain information around earnings and news.  
   - Typically use brokerage data not accessible to retail quants and focus on gross returns, so **Verdict: SKIP (background reading)**.

2. **Hendershott & Riordan; Kaniel, Saar & Titman** (institutional and retail order imbalance)  
   - Show that patient limit orders often supply liquidity and earn spreads, but the alpha is largely compensation for adverse-selection risk, not a free edge.  
   - Data are proprietary TAQ-level feeds; no clean path to CRSP/Polygon replication → **SKIP (background reading)**.

3. **Recent arXiv microstructure pieces (2022–2023) on queue positioning and limit-order placement**  
   - Useful for design intuition (e.g., how to avoid being “picked off”), but either short sample windows or simulated environments, no multi-asset panels, and no formal multiple-testing corrections → **SKIP (background reading)**.

None of these map to your TRIGGER conditions given the retail data constraint.

## Basket 2 — LLM-driven analysis of 10-K intangibles / risk disclosures (NOT 8-K)

### 2024+ scan outcome

There is active work on LLMs and financial text, but most fails on either **proper OOS** or **multiple-testing correction**, and many use proprietary embeddings or paid filings datasets:

- Many 2024–2026 arXiv q-fin papers apply LLMs to 10-K sections (MD&A, risk factors) but:
  - Use **2010–2020 only** with no **post-2020 OOS window**.  
  - Report **gross factor returns** ignoring transaction costs and realistic slippage.  
  - Concentrate in small/micro-caps or high-turnover portfolios (>300%/yr).  
  - Do not apply BH/FDR or White Reality Check across dozens of candidate signals.

- LLM methodology papers (e.g., **“Large Language Model Reasoning Failures”** on arXiv) focus on reliability of reasoning, not tradable signals, and thus do not pass your **net-return** and **multi-asset** filters.[8][32]

  - Strategy alpha is reported gross, with no detailed cost modeling, and the search space is vast with no explicit multiple-hypothesis correction.  
  - Uses specialized data interfaces beyond CRSP/Compustat.  
  - **Verdict: SKIP.** It’s an important cautionary example of how LLMs can overfit alpha mining without proper reality checks.

### Foundational SKIP (background reading)

1. **Pre-2024 textual sentiment + 10-K risk factor papers**

2. **Work on intangibles (R&D, brand, human capital) and valuation**

3. **LLM reasoning and robustness literature**  
   - Papers like **Large Language Model Reasoning Failures** catalogue where LLMs misreason.[8][32]  
   - Useful for model risk control but not alpha-specific and lack financial OOS validation → **SKIP (background reading).**

## Basket 4 — Factor decay 2025+ with OOS validation post-2020

This is the closest to your core question (alpha decay and OOS robustness), and there is active work, but it still falls short of your triage requirements in one or more dimensions.

### 2024–2026 evidence on factor decay and regime shifts

1. **AI-Driven Alpha Decay / homogenization**  
   - A 2026 arXiv paper summarized by The Neural Feed reports that widespread AI-driven quant adoption leads to **“algorithmic homogenization”** and accelerated alpha decay as signals become crowded and reflexive.[1]  
   - Framework is descriptive (homogenization, reflexive erosion) rather than tied to a single, implementable factor with full net-return accounting.  
   - Many signals are tested but without explicit multiple-testing corrections across the signal zoo.  
   - **Verdict: SKIP.**

2. **Momentum decomposition / persistence vs innovation**  
   - A 2026 paper **“Persistence and Innovation in the Momentum Signal”** (described in Capital Spectator’s “Research Review”) decomposes past returns into a predictable trend (persistence) and an unexpected innovation, arguing that most momentum profits are absorbed by risk factors, with alpha concentrated in unexpected shocks, especially in smaller and mid-tier stocks.[4]  
   - Uses long historical data, but details on net-of-cost returns, turnover, and multiple-testing controls are not fully specified in the public summary; we must assume they are incomplete relative to your filters.  
   - **Verdict: SKIP** until full OA paper can be examined.

3. **“The Many Facets of Stock Momentum: Distinguishing Factor and Stock Components” (2025, open-access)**  
   - Shows that momentum has both a **factor component** and a **stock-specific news component**, with investors underreacting to firm-specific information and possibly overreacting to systematic factors.[50]  
   - This helps explain **why some momentum signals deteriorate while others persist**, relevant to your paradigm failures.  
   - However:
     - The article focuses on momentum decomposition, not on a single tradable strategy with net returns and turnover fully modeled.  
     - Multiple-testing correction is absent across the different momentum variants considered.  
     - It does not provide a 15+ year sample with dedicated post-2010 or post-2020 OOS windows meeting your Filter 1 in a pre-registered sense.  
   - **Verdict: SKIP (background reading)** — conceptually useful, not directly triggering reactivation.

4. **“Momentum and Investors’ Lottery-Like Preference” (2025)**  
   - Examines how momentum interacts with lottery-like preference and finds that momentum profits are more pronounced among high-MAX stocks, especially small caps, with monthly returns ~2.26% for small high-MAX stocks.[24]  
   - This is strongly **small-cap concentrated**, arguably close to your Layer 2b failure mode (small-cap themed momentum).  
   - The paper reports gross returns and focuses on subsamples; transaction-cost modeling and multiple-testing corrections across MAX, size, and momentum dimensions are not explicitly detailed.  
   - **Verdict: SKIP** for implementation; useful as a cautionary reference.

5. **Practitioner factor views**  
   - JP Morgan **Factor Views 2Q 2026** and similar notes (Franklin Templeton, US small caps outlook) argue that some factors (value, small size) may be cyclically coming back as interest rates normalize and breadth widens, but these are practitioner outlooks, not formal academic OOS factor studies with full net-return and multiple-testing corrections.[10][21][30][54][55]  
   - **Verdict: SKIP (background reading).**

### Market regime and “value underperformance” thesis (Trigger 3)

- However, these are:

## Anti-pattern flags

These are items that superficially look promising but closely rhyme with your prior paradigm failures or violate one of your filters in ways that should be explicitly flagged.

1. **Small-cap and equal-weight momentum variants**  
   - The critique of Wen & Feng’s **“Winners Glide, Losers Stumble”** (SSRN, 2026) notes that a headline 8.5%/year alpha collapses to ~1.5%/year and becomes insignificant when portfolios are value-weighted rather than equal-weighted, with most of the “alpha” coming from micro/small caps.[11]  
   - This is an archetypal **small-cap themed momentum overfit**:  
     - High sensitivity to weighting scheme.  
     - Likely very high turnover.  
   - **Anti-pattern:** closely matches your Layer 2b failure (small-cap themed momentum with universe-concentration and overfit).

2. **High-turnover expectation-bias momentum**  
   - The LinkedIn critique of the **“Expectation Bias and Short-term Momentum in Stocks”** preprint highlights that the claimed alpha relies on ~97% *monthly* turnover, small-cap concentration, and confounded construction where expectation-bias measure is built from return history.[34]  
   - It also notes lack of rigorous multiple-testing correction and that net-of-cost alphas are not robust after realistic frictions.  
   - **Anti-pattern:** extreme turnover, small-cap concentration, and weak statistical discipline → avoid anything structurally similar.

3. **LLM-generated alpha mining without reality checks**  
   - **“Cognitive Alpha Mining via LLM-Driven Code-Based Evolution”** essentially automates factor mining using LLMs, raising high risk of **multiple-testing, data-mining, and overfitting** without White Reality Check or BH/FDR control.[9]  
   - This is a paradigm where an LLM “researcher” proposes and tests many value/quality/momentum-style picks — directly reminiscent of your Layer 2g failure (LLM-researcher value picks) but now extended to a wider factor zoo.  
   - **Anti-pattern:** automated signal generation with insufficient statistical discipline and no explicit post-2020 OOS validation.

4. **Lottery-like small-cap momentum (MAX-Momentum)**  
   - **“Momentum and Investors’ Lottery-Like Preference”** finds large momentum profits in small-cap high-MAX stocks.[24]  
   - This leans heavily into **lottery-like small caps**, where practical capacity and costs are problematic, and crowding/instability are likely.  
   - It strongly resembles your small-cap momentum failure mode and therefore *should not* be viewed as a new paradigm absent conservative net-return and capacity analysis.  
   - **Anti-pattern:** structurally vulnerable to overfit and cost drag.

5. **Nostalgic value narratives about small-cap revival**  
   - Several 2026 practitioner articles and Substack posts frame small-cap value’s prospective comeback in narrative terms (e.g., “after speculative growth leadership, disciplined value is back”).[49][21][30][52]  
   - These often **lack rigorous factor-level OOS validation** and rely on cyclical storytelling rather than statistically disciplined evidence.  
   - Given your Layer 2g failure and your explicit concern about “value-style nostalgia,” such narratives should be treated as **storytelling, not triggers**.  
   - **Anti-pattern:** value-style nostalgia and regime stories without formal post-2020 factor tests.
